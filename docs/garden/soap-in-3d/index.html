SOAP in 3D

Smooth Overlap of Atomic Positions turns a local atomic environment into a fixed-length vector that does not care about rotation or atom ordering. This page builds the idea in the order the maths does: density → basis → power spectrum → compression.

1 · The neighbor density

Put the central atom at the origin. Every neighbor contributes a smooth Gaussian. The sum is a density field: no atom labels, no orientation, just “how much stuff is where”. Drag to rotate; scroll to zoom.

2 · Expand the density

SOAP expands the density in a basis: a radial function for “how far” and a spherical harmonic for “which direction”. That gives coefficients cnlm. The basis is truncated at n_max radial functions and l_max angular channels — here 4 and 4.

ρi(r) = Σj exp(−|r − rij|² / 2σ²)
ρi(r) = Σnlm cnlm(i) gn(r) Ylm(r̂)
pnn′l(i) = Σm cnlm(i) cn′lm(i)

The power spectrum removes the m index, and with it the orientation. What is left is invariant under rotation, translation and permutation of identical atoms. Here n and n′ are both radial indices (0…n_max−1); the descriptor stores the upper triangle n′ ≥ n, giving one value per radial pair and angular channel l. Because dscribe uses real spherical harmonics, all cnlm and all pnn′l are real numbers — no complex conjugate needed.

2b · Radial basis functions gn(r)

The density is spread across all distances. SOAP asks: "how much density is near radius r₁? near r₂? near r₃?" Each radial channel gn(r) is a probe function peaked at a different distance. The projection of the density onto one probe gives a number; together they discretise the radial profile.

How to read the plot: each coloured curve is one gn(r). They tile the radial axis so that every distance within the cutoff is covered by at least one probe. A neighbor at distance r contributes to whichever gn peaks nearest to it.

Why Gaussians here? This page uses simple Gaussians evenly spaced between 0 and rc to make the idea visual. dscribe's real basis is a set of Gaussian Type Orbitals (GTOs), orthonormal under the SOAP inner product — but the intuition is the same: each basis function probes a different radial shell, and the coefficient cnlm tells you how much density in that shell has the angular shape Ylm.
Key insight: two environments with the same radial distribution but different angular arrangements give identical gn-projections but different cnlm for l > 0. That is why SOAP needs both radial and angular basis functions to distinguish environments.

3 · Spherical harmonics and where cnlm comes from

The density is a function of position. To turn it into numbers, SOAP projects it onto a basis: a radial function gn(r) that asks “at what distance?” and a spherical harmonic Ylm(θ,φ) that asks “in which angular shape?”. Their product is one basis function; integrating the density against it gives one coefficient cnlm.

Real spherical harmonics up to l=3. Red = positive, blue = negative regions of the function; the number of angular lobes grows with l. Each tile shows an equirectangular projection: the horizontal axis is the azimuthal angle φ ∈ [0, 2π] (like a world map's longitude) and the vertical axis is the polar angle θ ∈ [0, π] (latitude). For every radial channel n and angular channel (l,m) you get one real number (dscribe uses real spherical harmonics, so no imaginary part arises). The power spectrum pairs every n with every n′ ≥ n and sums over m:

pnn′l = Σm=-l..l cnlm · cn′lm
What cnlm physically means: think of it as a measurement result, not just a number. gn(r) selects a radial shell; Ylm(θ,φ) selects an angular shape (a lobe pattern). Multiplying them and integrating against the density asks: "how much density lives in shell n, arranged with angular shape (l,m)?" A large positive cnlm means the neighbor density is concentrated right where Ylm is large and positive; near zero means the density is symmetric with respect to that lobe pattern. The index m picks the orientation of the lobe within its family l — that is why a rotation changes m but cannot change l.
Why summing over m removes orientation: a rotation does not create or destroy lobes — it just relabels which m each lobe gets. Formally, rotating by angle α acts on the m-components of fixed l via a unitary Wigner D-matrix Dl(α): c'nlm = Σm' Dlmm'(α) cnlm'. The power spectrum is the dot product of two such vectors over m: pnn′l = cnl · cn′l. Because Dl is unitary, the dot product is unchanged — exactly the way a rotation of a 3D vector does not change its length. Summing over m effectively takes a "squared length" in orientation space, and lengths are rotation-invariant.

Try the sampling modes: the neighbor density ρ(r) is concentrated around atom positions — it is not spherically symmetric. So even a uniform grid gives non-trivial l>0 coefficients: each grid point is weighted by the local density value, which breaks symmetry wherever neighbors sit off-centre. Only a perfectly spherically symmetric density would make all l>0 coefficients vanish. Atomic-sites mode places one point per neighbor (the dimer has one neighbor → one point; that is correct).

Red points contribute positively, blue negatively. The integrand is ρ(r) · gn(r) · Ylm(r); the sum over samples times the cell volume is cnlm.

5 · Rotate the environment: c changes, the fingerprint doesn’t

Individual cnlm values are tied to the axes: rotate the neighbour geometry and they change. The power spectrum sums over m, so the fingerprint is unchanged. This is the invariance that makes SOAP usable as a descriptor.

The test cluster: a central atom (grey) with four asymmetric neighbors — one close along x, one above and to the left, one below, one far along z. The asymmetry is deliberate: a symmetric cluster (tetrahedral/octahedral) would give nearly zero l>0 coefficients and show little change on rotation.

The c values swing with the angle; the power spectrum stays put (up to grid integration error). Drag the slider to see the effect.

Equivariant vs invariant: cnlm is equivariant — rotating by R replaces each coefficient with a linear combination of the same-l coefficients via the Wigner D-matrix: cnlm → Σm′ Dlmm′(R) cnlm′. Rotations mix the m values within each l channel, but never move energy between channels. The power spectrum pnn′l = Σm cnlm cn′lm* contracts over m, so all m-dependence cancels out and p is invariant — the numbers on the left of this table don't change no matter how you rotate the cluster.

6 · Multi-element SOAP: one density field per species

With a single element you get one density field. With S elements SOAP builds one density field per element: ρZ(r) sums only over neighbors of species Z. Each field is expanded independently, giving a block of coefficients per species. The power spectrum then stores all pairs of blocks — that is what makes the descriptor grow quadratically with the number of elements.

Top half: the neighbor density split by element. Each colored blob is the Gaussian density for one species; atoms of different elements contribute only to their own field.

Bottom half: the power-spectrum block grid. Every cell is one (Z₁, Z₂) pair that the descriptor must store. With S species there are S(S+1)/2 upper-triangle blocks; each block has n_max²·(l_max+1) entries.

Why pairs? The power spectrum pZ₁Z₂nn′l cross-correlates the density of species Z₁ in radial channel n with the density of species Z₂ in channel n′. This captures whether atoms of type A and type B tend to co-occur in the same angular shell — information that a single mixed density would lose.
The cost: going from 3 to 20 species (a typical alloy dataset) multiplies the number of species-pair blocks by (20·21/2)/(3·4/2) = 70. That is the quadratic scaling that the mu1nu1 and mu2 compression schemes in section 8 address.

8 · Compression: from hundreds of numbers to a map

SOAP vectors are high-dimensional and grow with the number of species. The mu1nu1 scheme from Darby et al. compresses the species dimension: for 3 species it cuts 390 numbers to 240, and the length then scales linearly rather than quadratically with the number of elements.

Inside mu1nu1 — how the species dimension collapses

Uncompressed SOAP pairs every species-resolved radial channel with every other: with S species and nmax=4 radial functions there are S·4 channels, and the power spectrum stores the upper-triangle pairs of all of them. mu1nu1 (Darby et al., implemented in dscribe as compression={"mode":"mu1nu1"}) replaces the second factor of each product with a sum over all species: pZ₁,Z₂nn′l = Σm (cZ₁nlm)* · (Σz czn′lm). One species index survives, the pair structure collapses, and the length scales linearly with S instead of quadratically.

Why compress at all? The uncompressed power spectrum scales as (n_species·n_max)(n_species·n_max+1)/2·(l_max+1). mu1nu1 keeps species identity but removes the explicit species-pair cross terms; mu2 goes further and drops species labels entirely.

10 · What to remember

SOAP is a local fingerprint. It is invariant, fixed-length, and interpretable through its radial/angular channels. But it depends on r_cut, n_max, l_max, and on how species are weighted. Change the cutoff and you are describing a different environment.

11 · Local fingerprint vs global fingerprint

Everything in the 3D scene is a local SOAP vector: one descriptor for one central atom and its neighbours. A materials property model usually needs one vector per crystal, so the local vectors are combined into a global fingerprint.

psite 1local
psite 2local
…all sites
→
pglobalone vector per structure

The usual global descriptor is the average over sites:

pglobal = (1/Nsites) Σi psite i

Sometimes it is species-weighted, or concatenated with composition features. The invariance arguments are unchanged: each local vector is rotation-invariant, and so is the average.

Use local when…

you care about environments: clustering sites, detecting motifs, comparing coordination, or explaining which local geometry drives a prediction. It is also what you use for a single adsorbed molecule or surface site.

Use global when…

you train a structure-level property model (band gap, formation energy, refractive index). One vector per crystal is what scikit-learn/MODNet-style regressors expect; the cost is that site-level information is averaged away.

12 · SOAP similarity: comparing environments

Two environments A and B are similar if their power spectra point in the same direction. The SOAP kernel raises that cosine similarity to a power ζ, amplifying contrasts between nearly-identical and clearly-different environments.

K(A,B) = ( p_A · p_B / |p_A| |p_B| )ζ
K matrix — 11 preset environments
01

Reading it: diagonal is always 1 (self-similarity). Off-diagonal entries tell you how structurally similar two environments are according to SOAP. High ζ sharpens the kernel — slight angular differences get penalised more heavily, so only near-identical environments remain bright.

13 · Smoothness & cutoff sensitivity

Two key design choices affect how the descriptor responds to perturbations: the Gaussian width σ controls smoothness (larger σ → less sensitive to tiny atomic displacements), and the cutoff radius rcut controls which atoms matter.

Cutoff effect

The same environment described with rcut = 3, 4, 5 Å gives different descriptors because outer neighbors enter or leave. Below: cosine similarity between the descriptor at each cutoff and at rcut=5 Å.

Key insight: σ smooths over small perturbations — a large σ gives a more robust descriptor at the cost of angular resolution. The cutoff is a hard design choice: atoms just outside rcut are completely invisible to the descriptor. SOAP uses a smooth decay envelope near the cutoff to avoid discontinuities, but moving an atom from just inside to just outside still causes a finite change.
